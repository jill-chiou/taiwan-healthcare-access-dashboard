The problem

What do current metrics miss?

Taiwan usually compares healthcare resources with county-level rankings such as physicians per 10,000 residents. These metrics have two structural blind spots that hide the places that are actually underserved.

Blind spot 1: travel time is ignored
Within one county, reaching the nearest hospital from a remote township can take more than an hour on mountain roads. A county average erases that distance. Hualien ranks 3rd nationally in physician density, yet villages in its Zhuoxi and Fuli townships sit around the lowest 11% of all villages in accessibility.

Blind spot 2: averages hide within-county inequality
Urbanized counties such as Taoyuan and Miaoli look fine overall, but older residents stay in fringe townships while care concentrates in city centers. These places are administratively "urban", so they fall outside the official remote-area designation, even though access there is poor.

Method

E2SFCA: village level, real drive times

The Enhanced Two-Step Floating Catchment Area (E2SFCA) method estimates how much physician supply each place can realistically reach, accounting for competing demand. Instead of straight-line distance it uses road-network drive times from a self-hosted OSRM routing engine (18.1 million village–facility pairs) for all 7,616 villages. Scores are shown relative to the national average (1.0x).

Results

Where is access actually lacking?

96 high-risk villages in two groups: 82 villages with low access (below the national 25th percentile) and a large elderly population (at least 797 residents aged 65+, the national 75th percentile), plus 14 villages with zero access within 60 minutes. The county breakdown contradicts the common assumption that the problem is concentrated in the east.

Cluster analysis

K-means: six vulnerability profiles

353 townships clustered on three features: median E2SFCA score, share of residents aged 65+, and share of zero-access villages. K=6 was chosen by silhouette score (0.418, the highest for K≥3; K=2 only split remote from non-remote). Different profiles have different causes, so they call for different interventions.

Policy implications

Different vulnerabilities need different responses

The 96 high-risk villages are not one group. All 82 low-access × high-elderly villages are in townships that are not officially remote, while all 14 zero-access villages are in mountain indigenous townships. Allocating resources by county or by the remote-area label alone will keep missing the urban fringe.

▶ Limitations

1. Demand points are village centroids: the finest public resolution is the village, so polygon centroids stand in for population-weighted centers. This may understate travel times in large rural villages.

2. Geocoding misses are uneven: 7.3% of facilities in mountain indigenous townships could not be geocoded, vs 2.4% in non-remote townships. Missing rural facilities understate rural supply, so the urban–rural gap may be slightly overstated (impact on mountain-township supply < 8%).

3. Mobile clinics are not modelled: available mobile-clinic data does not match the 2024 analysis year. High-risk villages already served by mobile clinics may have better access than shown.

4. Offshore islands are excluded: 15 island townships have no road link to the main island. Their absence from the high-risk list reflects coverage, not safety.

5. Cross-sectional design: this is a 2024 snapshot and does not show change over time.
